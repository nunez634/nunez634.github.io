Exploring galactic age-color relationships

Spring 2025 | Principles of Remote Sensing

In this project, we calculated the color indices of spiral galaxies M81, M101, and NGC2841 to identify their star-forming regions. Done in collaboration with Wallace Astrophysical Observatory (thank you Dr. Person!).

Motivation

Stars form when massive lumps of space gas (called "molecular clouds", mostly made of hydrogen) collapse under their own gravity. The immense pressure at the core of the star heats the hydrogen gas to a high enough temperature for nuclear fusion to occur, signaling the start of the star's "main sequence," in which hydrogen is converted to helium. Depending on the mass of the star, its lifetime can vary . [Blue stars burn hotter and use up their fuel quickly.] Thus, an obesrved blue star is likely younger than a nearby red star, since blue stars tend to burn out faster. [hr diagram?] The most luminous stars in galaxies are hot, young, blue stars and (comparatively) cool, old, red stars. When we look at a faraway galaxy, we can use these clues to determine the regions which have the most active star formation: blue clumps have more active star formation, while redder areas have more inert[?] gas. The difference between the blue and red magnitudes is called the color index; We leveraged this principles to create maps of active star-forming regions in three different spiral galaxies.

Observation

We obtained data for this project at Wallace Astrophysical Observatory with 2 14" telescopes. Both telescopes were equipped with the Sloan U, G, R, I, and Z filters, which have minimal overlap in wavelength coverage. The telescopes are mounted with CMOS cameras, which have a highly reduced dark current compared to CCDs. We primarily used the Sloan g' (blue) and r' (red) filters to obtain the red and blue magnitudes needed to calculate the color index. We observed 6 galaxies in total: four spiral galaxies (M81, M101, NGC 2841, NGC 2403) and two elliptical galaxies (M87, M49). Our analysis focuses on M81, M101, and NGC2841; their stacked, calibrated images are shown below. Nice!

The images above are made of three separate frames from one filter all stacked to make sharp images of each galaxy.
